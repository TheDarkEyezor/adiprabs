## The dropout mindset

There's a question I struggled with internally over the last couple of months, which my first venture, Vani, really brought to the surface. It's about the freedom founders in this space usually take advantage of, and a subtle difference you don't always notice.

What do I mean by that? The culture in the startup world is now pushing entrepreneurial people to drop out of college early once they have a compelling idea, and to put all their effort into building it. That's being repeated across the world, not just in the US but in the UK as well, and it's led to the mindset that you're not a serious founder if you haven't dropped out already. I find that problematic.

Why? It's no secret by now that I'm an Indian immigrant. I was born in India, completed my primary and secondary school in Singapore, and came to the UK for university. Legally speaking, dropping out of my degree to work on a venture full-time would mean returning to India, because I hold an Indian passport. I would no longer have access to the community and the people who help these journeys take off in the UK, or in SF for that matter.

I don't think that's articulated enough. VCs have little reason to go looking for founders like us, because the most promising companies eventually come to them.

## "Then founding isn't for you"

I remember a YC talk Tom Blomfield gave at Imperial. During the Q&A, someone asked (and I'm sure he was interested in founding, and probably had an idea he wanted to push) what his recommendation would be for people who don't have the luxury of dropping out to focus full-time on their idea. Blomfield's response was basically: then founding isn't for you.

I found that a little disheartening. It isn't possible for me, or people like me, to just drop everything and focus on what we want. We've come to the MITs, the Stanfords, the Imperials, the Cambridges and Oxfords of the world to find this community, and because of the legal structure it's not trivial for us to take a gap year and work on what we want.

Nor is it an easy decision financially. As an international student I have to pay close to US$200,000, while a home student's fees are much lower, in the thousands. It's absurd. Of course there are reasons countries and universities impose such a massive difference in fees between international and local students, but that's not often discussed. And as someone who wants to be an entrepreneur, you even start getting questions like, well, why don't you drop out? Doesn't that just show you're not serious about founding? I found that a bit distressing. Not that it discouraged me, but that sentiment has the potential to turn anyone away from their entrepreneurial dreams.

## The only foreigner at the table

This really struck me while I was still actively working on Vani. I'd brought a cofounder in the UK on board, and we were attending a dinner put together by John from Strand Ventures (great guy, very friendly). At some point everyone started talking about schools in the UK, local schools whose names I'd never even heard of, and somehow everyone else at the table could hold a whole conversation about Queen Elizabeth Grammar School.

It hit me that I was the only foreigner at the table, the only one who had come in from another country. That shocked me, because it could mean one of two things. Either foreigners are just less likely to take risks, or the whole education and social system is structured so that foreigners can't come to the country and realise their dreams while studying. You have to complete your degree, get a full-time job, and then maybe you can consider starting your own venture. To me that seems fundamentally incompatible with the whole mantra of moving fast.

There was a period of a couple of months where I realised I wouldn't be able to drop out, or even take on contract work to earn money, and I felt completely immobile, stuck in my degree. It felt suffocating. It's so restrictive to have dreams that big, to have spoken with very talented, cracked builders who are willing to work with you, and to have your situation just not accommodate it.

## Where I've landed

That's what I was wrestling with for a while, but I've come to terms with it now. Unless there's a major legal shift in the way we receive immigrants, which is unlikely in the next couple of years, it just isn't possible for an international student to drop out of college and pursue their dream of founding a company.

So if anyone else finds themselves in this position, my best advice as things stand is to work on a side project. You'll have to balance your education with whatever you want to work on. If you're spending eight hours a day sleeping, then eight hours go on college and eight on your side project. Or 16 hours on your side project and zero on college, if you're that kind of academic. The point is that people have a higher threshold for taking us seriously, especially in a space where you can raise millions of dollars, so you need to work on something in parallel and show them you're worth the investment.

Once that investment comes through, once you've shown a proof of concept people can get behind and the money starts coming in, it becomes relatively easy to put your studies on hold, stay in the country, or even move to San Francisco to take your ideas further. Until then, it will just be a struggle. That's the unavoidable truth of the situation. You have to balance things, and unfortunately that means less recreation and less of a social life than we'd like. If you're okay with that, then by all means. But it's something you'll have to come to terms with, one way or another.
