I wanted to filter slop, AI-generated filler and rage-bait out of my X and LinkedIn timelines without shipping anything to a server. Browser extension, on-device inference, small enough to update over Chrome sync. Two independent Naive Bayes models — one trained on Davidson's hate/offensive-language corpus for rage, one on Hello-SimpleAI's HC3 human-vs-ChatGPT corpus for AI-text — plus a couple of heuristics and an optional Google Fact Check Tools lookup for misinformation.

Rage came together fine. AI-text was where the story is.

## The naive pass

First version was as bare as it gets: tokenize each document, take the log-odds of every token given the human-vs-ChatGPT label, keep the top-k by absolute score, ship the weights as a small JSON blob.

I turned it on and it flagged half my feed. Not the half I expected.

Anything written with a formal register got scored as AI. Any post that used "however", "moreover", "furthermore", or "additionally" tripped it. A friend's essay on urban planning — human, thoughtful, dense with discourse connectives — scored higher than the actual ChatGPT samples I'd tested it against. Careful academic writing looked, to the model, exactly like a language model trying to sound careful.

## The diagnosis

The model was not miscalibrated. It was, in the strictest sense, right. If you train a token-level classifier on HC3 and ask it which words shift the odds toward "written by ChatGPT," you get a list that is almost entirely composed of discourse connectives, hedges and evaluative closers: "however," "therefore," "moreover," "overall," "in conclusion," "notably." Those tokens really are more frequent in the ChatGPT half of HC3. That's not a bug in the data; it's a real property of the models that generated it.

The problem is that those same tokens are the ones a careful human writer reaches for when they want their sentences to hold together. The distribution overlaps too much where it matters. What the model had learned was a genuine signal — ChatGPT overuses discourse connectives — dressed up as a classifier, at a discrimination it couldn't do. On the tokens with the biggest log-odds, human formal writing and ChatGPT prose were nearly indistinguishable. The model wasn't wrong. It was useless.

## The fix, in three moves

**Move one — restrict the vocabulary.** Not every token that separates the distributions is a token worth trusting. I hand-picked around 40 candidates whose presence *actually* shifts my read of a passage — things I've watched ChatGPT lean on in a way careful humans don't, tokens like `certainly`, `delve`, `landscape`, `robust`, `holistic`, `seamlessly`, `comprehensive` — and threw everything else out of the training vocabulary. That's the `AI_ALLOW` set in `train_nb.py`:

```python
AI_ALLOW = {
    "certainly", "however", "therefore", "moreover", "furthermore",
    "additionally", "overall", "conclusion", "summary", "important",
    "notably", "comprehensive", "explore", "landscape", "insights",
    "consider", "ensure", "provides", "provide", "assist", "clarify",
    "generated", "model", "prompt", "synthetic", "deepfake", "dive",
    "delve", "navigate", "robust", "holistic", "seamlessly", "meanwhile",
    ...
}
```

Then `train_nb()` computes Laplace-smoothed log-odds on document frequencies over exactly that vocabulary, keeps the top 26 by absolute score, and writes the priors as counts scaled into 1–40. Anything not in the allow-list can't influence the score.

**Move two — apply the same trick to the rage model, in reverse.** The Davidson corpus is drawn from Twitter and is dense with slurs and generic profanity. Trained without care, the rage model collapses into a swear-detector: any post containing profanity scores as rage; a calmly delivered dehumanising claim scores clean. That's not useful. So the rage side of `train_nb.py` runs the tokenizer with a `BLOCK_TOKENS` set that strips out the generic profanity, plus a `RAGE_ALLOW` set of the tokens that actually mark rage-bait rhetoric — "sheeple," "globalists," "invasion," "replacement," "rigged," "censored," "traitors," "patriots," "hoax," "stolen," "propaganda." The rage documents are also pre-filtered to those containing at least one cue token from `RAGE_CUE_RE`, so the "positive" class isn't diluted by generic offensive tweets.

**Move three — compose, don't rely.** The NB scores don't decide anything alone. They feed into a small ensemble alongside slop heuristics (LinkedIn-buzzword regexes, em-dash density, opening phrases like "as an AI language model"), and misinformation goes through the Google Fact Check Tools API when the user provides a key, with a Wikipedia context fallback. Each detector can be toggled independently. If the AI classifier is over-firing on your feed, you can turn it off without losing the rage or slop filtering.

## What that bought me

- Discourse connectives on their own no longer flag a post. They contribute to a score, but they can't carry it.
- The rage model catches rhetoric, not vocabulary. Someone swearing at their landlord isn't rage-bait; someone calmly writing about "the great replacement" is.
- Because the priors are just token counts in a small JSON, the whole thing is transparent — you can open `models/nb_models.json` and see exactly which words push a score up and by how much. Language drifts ("delve" used to be neutral, now it's a tell) so `scripts/train_nb.py` is meant to be re-run.
- On-device, no network calls except the optional Fact Check API. `chrome.storage.sync` handles settings; the model weights ship with the extension.

## What I learned

Log-odds token classifiers over unigrams are the smallest useful thing you can build. That's a feature: it makes them easy to debug and easy to explain. It's also a trap. When the top-signal tokens overlap with normal, valuable behaviour — careful human prose in one case, ordinary swearing in the other — the model is behaving exactly as its training set demands, and it's still wrong for your use case. The fix isn't a bigger model. It's a smaller vocabulary.
